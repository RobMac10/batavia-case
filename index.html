What is agreed · from the 1629 records, and after

The Batavia was a ship of the Dutch East India Company, the VOC. It left the island of Texel, in the Netherlands, in late October 1628, bound for the city then also called Batavia, now Jakarta. Sources do not all agree on the exact day it left, or on the exact number of people on board at the start. Many lists say about 322 people were on the ship when it struck the reef.

In the early hours of 4 June 1629 the ship struck a reef in the Houtman Abrolhos, off the coast of what is now Western Australia. The journal of the commandeur, Francisco Pelsaert, opens on that Monday morning. About 40 people drowned at the wreck. The rest reached low islands nearby. Some later retellings give a much higher number for those first deaths. This file keeps the number recorded from the Dutch papers: about 40.

Pelsaert, the skipper Ariaen Jacobsz, and others left in the ship’s longboat to look for water, and then to find help. They reached the city of Batavia after a long voyage. Pelsaert came back in a ship called the Sardam, in mid-September 1629.

While they were away, a mutiny was led by the under-merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz. Many people were killed before the rescue. A group of soldiers, led by Wiebbe Hayes, found water on a higher island and held out. Hayes reached the rescue ship first.

Pelsaert wrote a long journal. The handwritten journal still survives in the Dutch National Archives. Other papers from the ship, including the lists of the crew’s pay, have been lost. The wreck was found in 1963. Timbers and objects from the ship are now in the Western Australian Museum.

No one who was there wrote a full day-by-day record of the weeks of the mutiny as they happened. Pelsaert was not on the islands during those weeks. What we know of them comes mainly from later questions, and from what he wrote down afterwards.

Francisco Pelsaert

Commandeur of the Batavia · Wrote the journal · Died 1630

The main written record of the wreck, the rescue, and the questions asked afterwards.

Source. Pelsaert’s journal, held in the Nationaal Archief, The Hague, VOC 1.04.02, inv. nr. 1098, folios 232–317. A silhouette is used. No contemporary portrait of Pelsaert is known for this file.

What they said. His journal opens on the morning the ship struck, 4 June 1629. He records the wreck, the landing on the islands, the longboat voyage for help, and his return on the Sardam. He also wrote down what people told him later about the weeks he had missed, and the decisions of the ship’s council after the rescue.

Could they really have seen it? He was there for the wreck, and for the rescue. He was not on the islands during the weeks of the mutiny. For those days he had to rely on what other people later said. A commandeur writing for the VOC also had to explain himself to his masters.

Why might they say it? To record the loss of the ship, to account for the cargo, and to set down what he had learned about the mutiny. A journal written for the company is still one person’s record. It is the best written source we have. It is not the only kind of evidence.

Wiebbe Hayes

A soldier on the Batavia · Led the group that held the higher island

The man who found water, kept his group together, and reached the rescue ship first.

Source. Hayes is known mainly through Pelsaert’s journal, which records what he had done and how he was later promoted. No book of Hayes’s own is known to survive. The journal sometimes spells his name Haijes. A silhouette is used. No contemporary portrait is known for this file.

What they said. We do not have a long statement in Hayes’s own hand. Pelsaert wrote that Hayes and his soldiers had found water and food on a higher island, that they had held out against the mutineers, and that they had captured Cornelisz. When the Sardam came, Hayes reached Pelsaert first. Pelsaert later made him a sergeant.

Could they really have seen it? Hayes was there for the weeks Pelsaert missed. He could see what happened on his own island, and in the fights with the mutineers. He could not see everything that happened on the other islands. What we read is mostly Pelsaert’s record of Hayes, not Hayes’s own page.

Why might they say it? If the journal is right, Hayes wanted the rescue ship to hear his group’s story first. After the rescue he was rewarded. We still have to remember that his voice comes to us through someone else’s journal.

The wreck of the Batavia

The ship itself, found in 1963 · Objects now in the Western Australian Museum

Silent evidence: timbers, a stone portico, coins, and other objects raised from the reef.

Source. The wreck was found on Morning Reef in 1963. From 1970, teams from the Western Australian Museum raised part of the stern and many objects. They can be seen in the Shipwrecks Museum in Fremantle. This card is the wreck, not a person. No living archaeologist is pictured.

What they said. The objects do not speak in words. They show that the ship was real, that it carried building stone and silver, and that it broke on that reef. They cannot, by themselves, tell us what was said on the islands, or fill in a day that no one wrote down.

Could they really have seen it? The wreck has sat on the reef since 1629. It can show us the ship. It cannot see the people on the islands. What we learn from it depends on how carefully it was raised, and on how we read it beside the journal, not instead of the journal.

Why might they say it? A wreck is not trying to persuade us. That is a strength. It is also a limit. Objects prove some things very well, and other things not at all.

What can the records still not tell us?

Why it still matters

The Batavia is often told as a thriller, or as a horror story. The file is still open because some papers from the ship were lost, because the commandeur was not there for the worst weeks, and because later tellings do not always agree. Learning to see what a record can prove, and what it cannot, is the work of this book.

Year 4 · Super Sleuths

Super Sleuths

Go further only if you want to. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added. Some harder parts of this story are kept in the Teacher Note, not here.

1. The night the ship struck

Pelsaert’s journal opens on Monday morning, 4 June 1629, the second day of Whitsuntide, with a bright, clear, full moon, on the reef of Frederick Houtman. The Western Australian Museum’s account of that night says a white gleam of breaking surf was seen, and that the skipper, Ariaen Jacobsz, as head of the watch, took it for moonlight.

The opening page of this case uses those documented facts, set in type. It is not a photograph of the journal, and it is not a whole translation of the entry.

2. Why the numbers do not match

Careful museum work often gives about 322 people on board when the ship struck, and about 40 who drowned at the wreck. A Western Australian Museum history page also says the ship left Texel on 29 October 1628 with 341 people. Other pages and books give 27 or 28 October, or “about 330.” The National Museum of Australia has said that about 100 people died in the immediate aftermath of the collision. That does not match the Dutch papers’ figure of about 40.

3. The weeks no commandeur was there to write down

Pelsaert left in the longboat and came back in mid-September. The worst weeks on the islands happened while he was away. His journal of those weeks is not a diary he kept on the spot. It is what he later wrote from questions, confessions, and other people’s memories.

4. Papers that really were lost

Pelsaert’s handwritten journal still survives in the Dutch National Archives (VOC 1098, folios 232–317). It is not right to say, as a fact, that “pages we know once existed” are missing from that journal, or that “statements stop mid-sentence,” unless a better source shows that. What has been lost, according to later museum research, includes the ship’s muster rolls and pay registers, which would have named the crew.

5. What the museum objects can prove

The wreck was found in 1963. In the 1970s the Western Australian Museum raised part of the stern, a carved stone portico, and many smaller objects. They prove the ship, and something of what it carried. They cannot, alone, tell us what was said on the islands.

6. Other Dutch marks on this coast

The Batavia was not the first Dutch ship on this coast. In 1616 Dirk Hartog left a pewter plate at Cape Inscription, on what is now Dirk Hartog Island. The plate’s short inscription is complete; it is not an unfinished fragment. In late 1696 Willem de Vlamingh landed on the island he named ’t Eylandt ’t Rottenest (Rottnest) and, in 1697, replaced Hartog’s plate with a new one, taking the old plate home.

Each voyage left a different kind of trace: a plate, a journal, a wreck and its objects. That is a better comparison than a claim that all the Dutch papers have the same “blank stretches.”

7. Two men left on the mainland

In mid-November 1629, after the trials on the islands, Pelsaert’s council left two men, Wouter Loos and Jan Pelgrom de Bye, on the Australian mainland. Their fate is unknown. Later stories about their lives there, including stories of contact with Aboriginal people of the mid-west coast, have not been proved. This file does not invent an ending for them.

If a class takes this question further, it should do so with care, and with guidance from the relevant Aboriginal communities of that coast, and from AIATSIS.

8. A book from 1647, and a journal from 1629

In 1647 a Dutch book, Ongeluckige Voyagie, van’t schip Batavia, was printed from Pelsaert’s journal. It was popular, and it was reprinted. A printed book is not the same thing as the handwritten journal in the archives. The 1647 engravings are old and useful, but they are pictures made in Europe, years later. Some of them are too hard for this year level.
